## Background & Context
The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow passage connecting the Persian Gulf with the Arabian Sea and the Indian Ocean, is one of the world's most critical maritime chokepoints. Roughly one-fifth of the world's total oil consumption and a substantial portion of global liquefied natural gas (LNG) pass through it daily. Its strategic importance has historically made it a flashpoint for geopolitical tensions, particularly between Iran and other global powers.

Over recent decades, the Strait has witnessed numerous incidents, including tanker seizures, attacks on shipping, and naval standoffs, often escalating global energy prices and threatening the stability of international trade routes. Iran, positioned along the northern shore, has periodically asserted its right to control passage, leading to calls for upholding the principle of innocent passage under international law. Oman, on the southern shore, has often played a mediating role in regional disputes, advocating for diplomatic solutions.

The necessity for a formal agreement arose from the perennial need to ensure uninterrupted and secure energy supplies to the global market, amidst fluctuating geopolitical landscapes and increasing concerns over maritime security in the region. This deal signifies a diplomatic effort to codify peaceful transit and minimize the risks associated with this vital waterway.
